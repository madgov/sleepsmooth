# 03 Clinical Pathway: from "I might have sleep apnea" to diagnosis and treatment

Research only. No code changed. Figures marked "not verified" were not confirmed against a primary source in this pass. Several figures come from secondary summaries of primary papers; those are labelled.

## Executive summary

1. SleepSmooth uses the NoSAS score (verified in `src/questionnaire/instruments/noSas.ts`; cited to Marti-Soler, Lancet Respir Med 2016). Its AUC was 0.74 in the population derivation cohort and 0.81 in a sleep-clinic validation cohort ([UNIL record](https://serval.unil.ch/notice/serval:BIB_25FFBF003632)). Independent sleep-clinic sensitivity and specificity vary widely (below).
2. Every questionnaire has good-to-moderate sensitivity and weak specificity. AASM's strong recommendation is that such tools must not replace PSG or HSAT for diagnosis ([AASM guideline](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/diagnostic-testing-OSA.pdf), via [secondary summary](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/aasm-guidelines-for-diagnostic-testing-for-obstructive-sleep-apnea-in-adults)). Our "cannot rule out" framing is on firm ground.
3. Licensing: ESS (Mapi Research Trust license required), STOP-Bang (owned by Dr. Chung/UHN; license required), and OSA-50 (permission needed) cannot safely be reproduced verbatim without permission. NoSAS and Berlin: no reuse terms found, so "free" is not verified.
4. USPSTF 2022 gives an "I" statement for asymptomatic adults, and it expressly does not apply to people who report symptoms ([AAFP summary](https://www.aafp.org/about/news/uspstf-sleep-apnea-final-2022)). The app should target symptomatic people and avoid "everyone should be screened" language.
5. Guideline-backed "see a clinician sooner" signals: excessive sleepiness affecting driving (NICE/DVLA), resistant hypertension, recurrent AF after cardioversion/ablation (AHA 2021), and vocational driving (NICE). The app can mention these as information, never as triage.
6. Roughly 80% of US OSA is undiagnosed per an AASM-published industry report (Frost & Sullivan), a figure that is old and US-specific (below).
7. Home testing (HSAT) is accepted only for uncomplicated, higher-risk adults. Cardiorespiratory disease, opioids and stroke point to in-lab PSG. The summary should surface these facts for the clinician, not decide the test.
8. Evidence that clinicians read patient-generated data is mixed: workload, trust and data quality are the stated barriers. A one-page, source-labelled, scannable summary is the safest design.
9. Follow-through evidence for OSA specifically is thin (one small trial, 30% vs 11% completion). Reminder and navigation evidence comes mostly from non-sleep settings.
10. Women and lean people are where questionnaires work worst. The app must say so, and must never show "low" as reassurance.

**Which can a free hackathon app include verbatim?** None of these is confirmed as freely redistributable. ESS, STOP-Bang and OSA-50 require permission or a license, and a free non-commercial app is not clearly exempt (ESS free-use categories are individual and non-funded academic; an app is neither, not verified). For NoSAS and Berlin, absence of a stated license is not permission. Recommended: keep NoSAS (cite, link, email the authors, record the outcome in `claims.ts`), and paraphrase nothing from licensed tools.

**Comparison with SleepSmooth.** NoSAS is short, needs only height, weight, neck, snoring, age and sex, and was derived on a population sample, which suits a no-clinic setting. Its weaknesses: it does not capture sleepiness, witnessed apneas, or hypertension, which are exactly the features AASM uses to choose HSAT eligibility. It also depends on neck circumference, which many people cannot measure accurately; the app already treats this as optional and flags incomplete scores. Sex is scored as male/female only, so the sex item should not be read as a risk judgment for women.

## 2. Guidelines and what they mean for wording

- **AASM 2017 (Kapur, JCSM 13(3):479-504).** Strong recommendation: clinical tools, questionnaires and prediction algorithms should not be used to diagnose OSA in the absence of PSG or HSAT ([secondary summary](https://speciality.medicaldialogues.in/aasm-guidelines-for-diagnostic-testing-for-obstructive-sleep-apnea-in-adults), [World Sleep Society](https://worldsleepsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Guidelines-Paper_Adult-OSA_draftB.pdf)). Testing goes with a comprehensive sleep evaluation. HSAT is an accepted alternative for uncomplicated adults at increased risk of moderate-to-severe OSA, defined as excessive sleepiness plus at least two of: habitual loud snoring, witnessed apnea/gasping/choking, diagnosed hypertension. PSG rather than HSAT is advised with significant cardiorespiratory disease, neuromuscular weakness, hypoventilation, chronic opioid use, stroke, or severe insomnia; a negative or inadequate HSAT should lead to PSG ([AASM-hosted payer scorecards](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/eviCore_Revised_Scorecard_June_2017.pdf)). I could not retrieve the full guideline text (PDF unreadable to the fetch tool), so wording is from AASM-hosted summaries.
- **USPSTF 2022.** "I" statement: insufficient evidence on benefits and harms of screening asymptomatic adults or adults with unrecognized symptoms. It does not cover people who report symptoms such as snoring or sleepiness, nor children, adolescents or pregnant people ([AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/about/news/uspstf-sleep-apnea-final-2022); [USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/obstructive-sleep-apnea-in-adults-screening)). Treatment trials showed improved sleepiness but no significant mortality or cardiovascular benefit from PAP.
- **NICE NG202 (2021).** Use ESS for preliminary sleepiness assessment, consider STOP-Bang alongside it, and do not use ESS alone to decide on referral because not everyone with OSAHS is sleepy. The committee noted STOP-Bang concerns in women ([NICE rationale](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng202/chapter/Rationale-and-impact)).

**Wording implications.** (a) Frame the app as for people who already notice symptoms, not mass screening. (b) Never say "screening shows low risk"; say "this questionnaire cannot rule out sleep apnea." (c) Say that only a sleep test ordered by a clinician can confirm or exclude it. (d) Do not tell users which test (HSAT vs PSG) they need; list facts for the clinician instead. (e) Say a normal score does not reduce the value of discussing symptoms.

## 3. Red flags tied to prompt evaluation (only guideline-explicit items)

- **Sleepiness that affects driving.** NICE's rationale cites DVLA guidance: drivers with suspected or confirmed OSAHS and excessive sleepiness affecting driving must not drive until symptoms are controlled; referral should include DVLA information; vocational drivers (bus, train, lorry, pilots) should be offered early assessment ([NICE](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng202/chapter/Rationale-and-impact)). This is UK-specific; no US equivalent verified. The repo already carries a CDC-sourced line (`claims.ts` line 116); verify that URL is cited in the UI.
- **Resistant/poorly controlled hypertension, recurrent AF after cardioversion or ablation, pulmonary hypertension.** AHA 2021 statement recommends OSA screening in these groups; a sleep study should be considered in heart failure, tachy-brady syndrome, ventricular tachycardia and cardiac arrest survivors when symptoms are present ([ACC summary](https://www.acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2021/06/25/15/07/obstructive-sleep-apnea-and-cvd); [HME News](https://hmenews.com/article/aha-releases-statement-on-osa-and-cardiovascular-disease)). The 2023 ACC/AHA AF guideline wording on sleep apnea: not verified.
- **Comorbidities affecting test choice** (cardiorespiratory disease, stroke, opioids): AASM, above.
- **Crash risk context.** Untreated OSA is associated with crash risk in commercial drivers ([Tregear meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2792976/)), but a review noted self-reported sleepiness and OSA severity predict crashes poorly. Do not use it to rank drivers.

**Safe phrasing.** "Guidelines from [source] suggest people with [condition] talk with a clinician about a sleep evaluation. This is general information, not an urgent-care assessment." Present as a clinician-discussion checklist the user can tick, not an app-generated alert, and never present absence of flags as reassurance. No emergency triage rules should be added.

## 4. Care pathway and barriers

Typical path: symptoms, primary care or direct-to-specialist visit, comprehensive sleep evaluation, HSAT or in-lab PSG, diagnosis, treatment, follow-up (AASM above; NICE NG202 for UK).

Treatments: PAP (AASM-linked guidance supports PAP for sleepy adults, [payer summary](https://www.bcbsnd.com/providers/policies-precertification/medical-policy/diagnosis-and-treatment-of-obstructive-sleep-apnea-for-adults)); oral appliances (joint AASM/AADSM 2015 guidance, content not retrieved); positional therapy as secondary or adjunct ([AASM at-a-glance](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/MedicalTherapyOSA-Guideline-at-a-Glance.pdf)); tirzepatide (Zepbound), FDA-approved 20 Dec 2024 for moderate-to-severe OSA in adults with obesity, alongside diet and activity, based on two trials in 469 adults ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-medication-obstructive-sleep-apnea)); AASM said it only works for obesity-related OSA and may not cure it ([AASM statement](https://aasm.org/zepbound-approval-sleep-apnea-american-academy-sleep-medicine-statement)); hypoglossal nerve stimulation as second-line for moderate-to-severe OSA after PAP intolerance, with payer BMI cutoffs varying (35 or 40 kg/m2) ([AAO-HNS position via review](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4FD2B80D4B4D04DEC66E2A4C27ECCCCA/S0022215125000295a.pdf/hypoglossal-nerve-stimulation-in-patients-outside-the-star-trial-criteria-a-systematic-review.pdf)). The app should list options for discussion only and never recommend one, per project rules (no changes to treatment settings or medication).

**Underdiagnosis.** AASM-published Frost & Sullivan report: about 12% US adult prevalence, about 80% undiagnosed (5.9 million diagnosed, 23.5 million undiagnosed) ([AASM](https://aasm.org/resources/pdf/sleep-apnea-economic-crisis.pdf)). Global: 936 million adults aged 30-69 with mild-to-severe OSA (95% CI 903-970 million), a ResMed-funded modelling study ([Benjafield, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31300334/)). A ResMed release cites ">85%" undiagnosed ([ResMed](https://investor.resmed.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/113/the-lancet-publishes-more-than-936-million-have-obstructive-sleep-apnea-worldwide)). Both are industry-linked and not independently reconciled. Cite as "estimates".

**Wait times and cost.** No current US wait-time or price data found: not verified. Older, non-US data: Singapore trial of a home device, diagnosis in about 21 vs 80 days, PAP started in about 46 vs 118 days ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/what-can-reduce-cost-time-to-diagnosis-in-obstructive-sleep-apnea-)). Canadian 2014 pricing: about $419 home vs $746 in-lab; Spanish trial: home study saves about $450 ([Springer summary](https://www.springermedicine.com/sleep-apnea/polysomnography/reducing-cost-and-time-to-diagnosis-and-treatment-of-obstructive/21772254)). Do not show these as US prices; say "costs and waits vary; ask the clinic and your insurer."

## 5. What clinicians want in a patient-provided summary

Direct evidence on OSA-specific patient summaries: none found: not verified. Evidence on patient-generated health data (PGHD) generally:
- Barriers include workload, safety concerns about data overload, trust in data quality, and lack of EHR integration ([qualitative study of 20 primary care clinicians](https://i-jmr.org/2013/2/e29/PDF); [JMIR 2026](https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e86368/PDF)).
- A systematic review of 13 studies found clinicians struggle to engage with patients' wish to have their data used ([Lordon et al.](https://digitalcommons.tacoma.uw.edu/nursing_pub/204)).
- A six-stage workflow (align objectives, judge quality, evaluate utility, reformat, interpret, decide) frames what clinicians must do with PGHD ([Southampton](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/419930/1/Common_barriers_to_the_use_of_patient_generated_data.pdf)).

Inference (not from a clinician survey): a one-page summary that is quick to scan, separates user-reported from device-observed items, states data quality and gaps, and does not interpret the findings will cost the clinician the least time. Items AASM and NICE use to structure evaluation (sleepiness, loud snoring, witnessed apnea/choking, hypertension, comorbidities, driving, medications such as opioids) should be asked as fields. Likely reasons to ignore a summary (inference): long raw logs, diagnostic language, unlabeled sources, unstated accuracy limits.

## 6. Follow-through

- A small Iranian randomized trial (104 participants, 2-month follow-up): educational booklets raised sleep-assessment attendance to 30% vs 11% control; main barriers were time, symptom improvement and test cost ([PMC7790540](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7790540/)).
- Non-sleep evidence: navigator phone calls cut cancer-clinic no-shows from 17.5% to 10.2% ([BIDMC](https://research.bidmc.org/alexa-kimball/publications/patient-navigation-based-predictive-modeling-decreases-no-show-rates-cancer-care)); a rapid review found reminders and navigators reduce no-shows ([Manchester](https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/predictive-model-based-interventions-to-reduce-outpatient-no-show/)). Transfer to OSA testing is untested.
- HSAT programs report drop-off at equipment pickup and test performance ([Health Catalyst, vendor](https://healthcatalyst.com/learn/success-stories/sleep-apnea-digital-patient-engagement)); no completion rates verified.
- Not found: any evidence that a "find a sleep center" locator or conversation script raises testing rates. Treat those as plausible, unproven.

## 7. Special populations and equity

- **Women:** often report fatigue, insomnia, mood symptoms and poor sleep quality rather than loud snoring; one 2026 review estimates up to 75% of women with OSA are undiagnosed ([Pulm Ther review via search](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8461585)). A questionnaire-by-sex accuracy comparison was not found: not verified. NICE flagged STOP-Bang concerns in women.
- **BMI under 35 / lean:** STOP-Bang performed near chance in a young, leaner referred cohort (AUROC 0.52); lowering the BMI threshold trades specificity for sensitivity ([Korean modified STOP-Bang, summary](https://sleepmedres.org/journal/view.php?number=154)). No community prevalence figure for lean people was found. NoSAS gives BMI 25-<30 only 3 points, so lean people with OSA can score "below".
- **Children:** out of scope. AAP guidance says children should be screened for snoring and that polysomnography is the preferred test ([AAP 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22926173/)). The app should say it is for adults and direct caregivers to a pediatrician.
- **Pregnancy:** USPSTF excludes it; Berlin and ESS perform poorly in pregnancy.
- **Accessibility:** keep text readable, no neck-measurement dependency, metric and imperial units (already supported), screen-reader labels, and plain-language cost and access notes.

## 8. Ranked recommendations (all within the safety rules)

1. **Add the AASM-style HSAT-eligibility facts as optional "tell your clinician" fields** (sleepiness, loud snoring, witnessed apneas/choking, hypertension) using plain in-house wording, not copied from ESS or STOP-Bang. Show them in the summary as user-reported facts, not as a score or test recommendation.
2. **Replace any "low/below cutoff" display with "cannot rule out" wording**, with no green colour and no reassurance copy, citing AASM/NICE on questionnaire limits.
3. **Resolve licensing now:** email the NoSAS authors, log the answer, and do not add ESS, STOP-Bang or OSA-50 text without written permission. Keep the NoSAS citation visible.
4. **Add a "clinician conversation script" and one-page printable summary** (user-reported / audio observation / missing data / questions to ask). Label as unvalidated design based on PGHD barriers, not on a clinician survey.
5. **Optional comorbidity checklist with citations** (resistant hypertension, recurrent AF after procedure, heart failure, stroke, opioid use, driving sleepiness) phrased "guidelines suggest discussing a sleep evaluation", never "you are at risk".
6. **Drowsy-driving information card** citing CDC and, for UK users, DVLA via NICE; "this is general information, not triage."
7. **Women, lean-body and older-adult caveat panel** shown to everyone: "symptoms can look different; a low score does not mean you do not have OSA."
8. **Follow-through tracker without nagging:** user-set reminders for "book appointment" and "ask about test type", with a fact card on barriers (time, cost, symptoms improving) from the one trial; present as unproven aid.
9. **Cost and wait transparency card:** "costs and waits vary; ask clinic and insurer", with questions to ask (self-pay price, HSAT vs PSG, telehealth options). No US numbers until verified.
10. **Adults-only gate with pediatric and pregnancy exits** (AAP and USPSTF scope statements), plus a treatment-options explainer (PAP, oral appliance, positional, tirzepatide for obesity-related OSA, HGNS) labelled "options to discuss, not advice."
